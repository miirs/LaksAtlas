Challenges of Living in Water

Water as a physical and chemical medium, gas exchange and diffusion, freshwater vs. saltwater osmotic challenges, and how acidification and water treatment shape fish physiology.

💧
Water as a Medium
Polarity · hydrogen bonds · heat capacity · viscosity
▶

A water molecule (H2O) is a polar covalent molecule: oxygen's higher electronegativity pulls the shared electrons closer to itself, and the roughly 105° angle between the two hydrogen atoms gives the molecule a permanent charge asymmetry, a negative oxygen end and positive hydrogen ends. This is the source of hydrogen bonding, weak attractions that constantly form and break between water molecules.

In liquid water these bonds are transient. Below freezing they lock into a rigid lattice, ice, which is less dense than liquid water because the fixed lattice spaces molecules further apart. This is why ice floats, and it is fairly unique among common substances.

Water's phase transitions define the Celsius scale (0°C freezing, 100°C boiling at sea-level pressure; boiling point drops with altitude). Freshwater is densest at 4°C, lighter both above and below that point, and dissolved ions shift both density and freezing point.

Two more physical properties matter enormously for aquatic life: high heat capacity (water resists temperature change, so it warms and cools slowly, lagging air temperature by weeks or months, e.g. coastal sea-surface temperatures are often coldest in March), and high viscosity (resistance to flow), which shapes body form and gas-exchange organ design because moving through water is energetically expensive.

🌊
Stratification: Lakes & Fjords
Thermocline · turnover · sill fjords · anoxic bottom water
▶

Lakes stratify seasonally as temperature-driven density differences layer the water column. In winter, ice cover blocks both gas exchange with the atmosphere and wind-driven mixing. In spring and autumn, once the whole column reaches a uniform 4°C, density differences vanish and the lake fully mixes, called turnover. Summer heating then re-stratifies the lake, forming a thermocline, the sharp transition layer between warm surface water and colder, denser water below.

Oxygen follows this same layering. Bottom water and sediment, cut off from atmospheric exchange, can become hypoxic (oxygen-poor) or anoxic (oxygen-free) as respiration consumes oxygen faster than it is replenished.

Fjords stratify too: incoming river freshwater is much lighter than salty seawater, forming an outflowing brackish surface layer that drives a compensating inflow of colder, saltier water at depth. A sill fjord, with a shallow underwater threshold near its mouth, can trap this deep basin water with very limited exchange, sometimes leading to permanent or seasonal bottom hypoxia.

Extreme natural examples exist: the Black Sea and Baltic Sea have permanently anoxic bottom water, while the Red Sea and Gulf of Mexico show seasonal hypoxia, increasingly worsened by human-driven eutrophication and climate change.

Gases in Water & Diffusion
Partial pressure · Fick's Law · solubility vs. temperature
▶

The atmosphere is roughly 78% nitrogen (N2), 21% oxygen (O2), with the remainder trace gases including CO2 (~0.04%). Atmospheric pressure at sea level is about 101.3 kPa, falling roughly 1 kPa per 80 m of altitude. Each gas contributes its own share, its partial pressure, to the total.

At equilibrium, a gas's partial pressure is the same in water as in the air above it, regardless of temperature or salinity. The actual concentration of dissolved gas molecules is not constant, though: it falls as temperature or salinity rises, because warmer, saltier water simply holds fewer gas molecules per volume at the same partial pressure.

Fick's Law describes diffusion rate: J = D·A·(C1−C2)/X. Of these variables, A and X are the two an organism (or an aquaculture engineer) can actively manipulate to change exchange capacity, by growing more gill surface area or thinning the diffusion barrier.

Because gas solubility falls as temperature rises while a fish's metabolic oxygen demand rises with temperature too, warm water pulls fish physiology in two opposing directions at once, less oxygen available exactly when more is needed.

🫧
Oxygen, CO2 & the Bicarbonate Buffer
Respiration · hemoglobin · the bicarbonate system
▶

Respiration converts biochemical energy using O2 as the final electron acceptor: C6H12O6 + 6O2 → 6CO2 + 6H2O (+ ATP). This reaction in mitochondria does not always run cleanly to completion, producing reactive intermediates called reactive oxygen species (ROS) or free radicals, kept in check by antioxidant enzyme systems.

Water holds far fewer O2 molecules per volume than air, generally under 4% as many at equilibrium. To supply metabolically active tissue, fish rely on hemoglobin, an iron-containing (heme) transport protein in red blood cells that binds O2 in weak, reversible bonds. This lets far more oxygen be carried per volume of blood than could exist as free dissolved gas, while still preserving the partial-pressure gradient that keeps diffusion moving.

CO2 is far more soluble in water than O2 or N2 because it reacts chemically with water rather than just physically dissolving: CO2 + H2O ↔ H2CO3 ↔ HCO3⁻ + H+. This is the bicarbonate buffer system, the most important inorganic pH buffer in both natural water and the body. The reaction is naturally slow (the enzyme carbonic anhydrase speeds it up enormously in vivo), and its equilibrium position shifts with pH.

Nitrogen (N2) is biologically inert but its low solubility matters practically: warming or increasing salinity can push water into gas supersaturation before the excess N2 has time to diffuse back out, a real hazard in aquaculture water systems.

⚗️
Water Chemistry: pH, Ions & Humus
Kw and pH · Na+/Cl− · Ca2+/Mg2+ buffering · conductivity
▶

Water self-ionises: 2H2O ↔ H3O+ + OH−, with equilibrium constant Kw = 1.0×10⁻¹⁴. Taking −log[H3O+] gives the pH scale, where 7 is neutral. Rainwater is naturally slightly acidic (around pH 5.6) from reacting with atmospheric CO2; seawater is basic thanks to its high carbonate/bicarbonate content, built up over geological time from weathered limestone.

The dominant seawater ions are Na+ and Cl−, which over geological time built up to today's ~30–36‰ salinity. Divalent cations Ca2+ and Mg2+ vary widely in freshwater depending on catchment geology, whether it drains limestone or acidic, slow-weathering bedrock, and directly control a water body's buffering capacity.

Total ion content can be estimated quickly by measuring conductivity (µS/cm or mS/m). Norwegian freshwater typically runs 0.4–15 mS/m, reflecting generally ion-poor bedrock.

Humus (measured as total or dissolved organic carbon, TOC/DOC) consists of weak organic acids from decomposed matter. It browns water, lowers pH, and can bind and transport metals and organic pollutants, an important complication for water treatment and disinfection in aquaculture.

🧂
Freshwater vs. Saltwater: the Osmotic Squeeze
Chloride cells (MRC) · calcium's protective role
▶

A freshwater bony fish is, physiologically, "a bag of ions swimming in almost pure water": its body fluids sit around 300 mOsm, while the surrounding water is close to 0–10 mOsm. Osmosis constantly drives water into the fish and ions out of it, especially across the large, thin, highly permeable gill surface.

Fish counter this with a very dilute urine to void the excess water, while salts that are still lost must be actively reclaimed. Specialised chloride cells (also called mitochondria-rich cells, MRC) on the gill surface, powered by Na-K-ATPase, Mg2-ATPase and carbonic anhydrase, handle this energy-costly active ion uptake.

Water chemistry directly affects how hard this is: higher ion content (higher conductivity) makes uptake cheaper, and calcium plays an outsized protective role by stabilising membranes and reducing how leaky they are to water and ions. Extremely calcium-poor water, such as high-mountain lakes, can limit fish survival even with zero pollution; trout can struggle when Ca2+ falls below about 0.5 mg/L for long periods, while Arctic char tolerates lower calcium levels than brown trout. In very ion-poor water, fish can partly compensate by increasing chloride-cell density on the gills.

☠️
Ecotoxicology Basics
NOEC/LOEC/LC50 · additive vs. synergistic effects · BLM
▶

Three thresholds define how toxicity is measured: NOEC (No Observed Effect Concentration, the highest dose producing no measurable harm), LOEC (Lowest Observed Effect Concentration, the lowest dose that does), and LC50 (the concentration lethal to 50% of test fish within a set exposure window, commonly 96 hours).

Real exposures are mixtures, not single substances, and combined effects can be additive (effects simply sum), synergistic (worse than additive, a "cocktail effect"), or antagonistic (less than additive). For example, inorganic aluminium and certain pesticides can jointly damage gill enzymes even at doses that would each be sub-lethal alone.

Toxicity is not fixed: fish tolerate short high-concentration spikes better than the same total dose spread over continuous long-term exposure, and effects are generally weakest near a fish's optimal temperature and a near-neutral pH (6.5–7.5), while low oxygen, high CO2, and low calcium/hardness all tend to make toxicity worse.

The biotic ligand model (BLM) predicts a metal's toxicity from its binding affinity (log K) to a biological surface like gill tissue, weighed against competition from other ions and organic matter (like humus) already in the water. The silver ion is thought to be the single most toxic metal ion studied; aluminium is the best-documented case in Norwegian waters because of acid rain.

🌧️
Acidification & Aluminium Toxicity
Al speciation · smolt sensitivity · mixing zones
▶

Acid rain forms when sulphur and nitrogen compounds from fossil-fuel combustion react in the atmosphere to form sulphuric and nitric acid, pushing rainfall well below its natural pH of ~5.6. Acidified water itself is usually not the core problem for fish, it is the metals it mobilises from soil and bedrock, above all aluminium.

Al3+ dominates in acidic water and, due to its high charge and small ionic radius, binds strongly to and damages negatively-charged gill surfaces. Toxicity depends heavily on chemical form ("speciation"): labile aluminium (LAl / Ali), mostly inorganic hydroxide forms, is the toxic fraction, while aluminium bound to organic matter (humus) is far less toxic.

H+ increases gill membrane permeability, causing passive ion leakage, while aluminium separately poisons the enzyme systems (carbonic anhydrase, Na-K-ATPase) that drive active ion uptake in chloride cells. Together these mechanisms can cause rapid, sometimes fatal, salt loss.

Atlantic salmon smolt are exceptionally vulnerable in the weeks before seawater migration: the freshwater isoform of Na-K-ATPase (α1a) is being replaced by a seawater isoform (α1b) that is extremely aluminium-sensitive. A brief acid/Al exposure can suppress this enzyme for weeks, reducing seawater survival even without any freshwater mortality, partly because affected smolt linger longer in estuaries, more exposed to sea lice and predators.

A further complication is the mixing zone: when acidic, aluminium-rich water mixes with neutral or limed water, Al briefly polymerises into intermediate, highly gill-reactive forms before eventually settling into harmless, inert particles. Water that looks safe once fully mixed can be acutely toxic for the first few minutes, a temperature-dependent window, roughly 3 minutes at 16–18°C versus over an hour at 5–6°C.

Water Treatment in Aquaculture
Liming · silicate lye · target pH 6.5–6.7
▶

Because raw pH alone does not define toxicity (see mixing zones above), the practical target for smolt production water is neutralising to pH 6.5–6.7, which raises pH and builds calcium/buffering capacity, actively reducing aluminium's toxicity rather than just its concentration.

MethodNotes
Limestone productsCrushed limestone, lime slurry, shell sand filters, chalk. Adds calcium and buffering capacity
Lye / soda (NaOH, Na2CO3)Raises pH but lacks calcium's protective effect. Overdosing risks forming toxic aluminate
Silicate lye (Na4SiO4)Most widely used in Norwegian smolt facilities. Rapidly binds low-molecular Al, preventing the mixing-zone reaction, effective even at pH 5.8–5.9
Seawater (~3%)Raises pH, calcium and ionic strength together

Flowing water systems are hardest to treat because they can never stop and must stay chemically stable even in cold conditions, which slow the neutralisation reaction. If silicate lye is combined with seawater dosing, the silicate must always be added before the seawater, to avoid creating an estuarine-style mixing zone inside the fish tanks themselves.

⚠️
Toxic Metals Beyond Aluminium
Fe, Mn, Zn, Cd, Cu, Co, Ni, Pb, Cr · life-stage sensitivity
▶

Most metals act as ions in water; their binding to gill surfaces and uptake into the body depend on charge and ionic radius, and some enter through ion channels meant for essential nutrients they chemically resemble.

Different metals matter for different reasons: iron (Fe) and manganese (Mn) tend to be more of a physical clogging/precipitation problem on gills than pure enzyme poisons. Zinc (Zn) and copper (Cu) are essential micronutrients at trace levels but toxic in excess, and copper is unusual in being roughly equally toxic in both seawater and freshwater. Cadmium (Cd), cobalt (Co), nickel (Ni), lead (Pb) and chromium (Cr) round out the metals routinely tracked in Norwegian water-quality assessments.

Sensitivity varies by species, strain and life stage in a pattern similar to aluminium: salmon eggs are most vulnerable around hatching, when the pH-sensitive hatching enzyme chorionic dehydrogenase is active; yolk-sac fry show extended yolk absorption and higher deformity rates under poor water quality; and, as with acidification, the smolt stage remains the single most sensitive life stage overall.
